Seating for group work: pairs, tables and pods

The seats you choose decide who works together. A table of four can be one group of four, two pairs, or four students working alone — and each needs different seating.

Pick the group size first

  • Pairs keep everyone involved — there's nowhere to hide.
  • Threes add a third view without losing focus.
  • Fours suit projects with roles, but one student often goes quiet.

Tables of four as two pairs

A common setup: students sit at tables of four but work in pairs for most tasks, joining as four for bigger ones. Decide which two seats form each pair — usually side by side — and plan each pair, not just each table.

Mix each group

Balance each group on what matters for the work: a confident reader with a developing one, an English learner with a supportive speaker, a mix of boys and girls. Avoid putting all the strongest students together.

Partners on purpose

Some students should work together — a reading buddy, a translator, a calm partner for a student who struggles. Plan those pairs first, then fill around them.

Rotate partners

Changing groups every few weeks spreads relationships across the class. Keep track of who has worked together so the next chart gives everyone new partners.

In ClassMap

Split any table into pairs, threes or solo seats, mark students who should be in the same group, tag students (for example "Needs support" and "Strong reader") so each one sits with a helper, and avoid partners from your last charts automatically.
